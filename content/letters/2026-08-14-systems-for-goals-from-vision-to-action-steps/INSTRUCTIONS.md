## Model
Use the AUDIT model to move from stuck to steady.

A: Assign one goal. Pick one big goal that matters to you. Write it in clear terms with a number and a deadline. Example: “Save $5,000 by December 31” or “Run a 10K by June 1.” If the goal is about a relationship, write what success looks like, like “Call my brother every Sunday for 3 months.” This step stops you from chasing many goals at once.

U: Unpack the goal into milestones. Break the goal into 3 to 5 smaller targets. Ask what must be true at the halfway point and at the quarter points. For the savings goal, set milestones like $1,000 by March 31, $2,500 by June 30, and $4,000 by September 30. For the run, set milestones like run 3 miles by April 1 and run 5 miles by May 1. This step turns a big dream into checkpoints.

D: Date each milestone. Put a deadline on every milestone. A milestone without a date is just a wish. Write the date next to each target. Then work backward to the present. Ask what must be done this week to hit the first milestone. This step gives you a clear path from today to the finish line.

I: Insert the actions into your week. Under each milestone, list the tasks or habits you need. For savings, that may be “Move $500 on the 1st” and “Eat out once a week.” For the run, that may be “Run 3 times a week” and “Rest on planned days.” Put these tasks into your calendar or weekly plan. This step makes the goal part of your normal life, not an extra wish.

T: Track and adjust weekly. Set a short review time, like Sunday evening. Check what you completed, what you missed, and what the numbers show. If you are on track, keep going. If you are off track, change one action or move one milestone. This step completes the lesson by turning your plan into a living system that keeps you moving.

## Introduction

You have a goal that has been sitting in your head for too long. It may be a number, a body, a business, a relationship, a skill, or a deadline. You think about it when you are tired, bored, or waiting for life to feel more meaningful. You want it. But when you sit down to work on it, you cannot name the next step.

That is the main tension of this lesson. The goal is not stuck because you are lazy. It is stuck because it has no shape. A goal with no plan is a wish. A wish can feel important. It can even feel urgent. But it does not move. It waits for motivation, a free weekend, or the right mood. When work, family, or health takes the first slot in your week, the goal slips again.

The truth is simple: a goal becomes real when it has a number, a date, and a first task.

The desired outcome is also simple. In 7 days, you will have one written goal plan with a deadline, three milestones, and the first task completed. You do not need to give up your job, your family time, or your budget. You need to give the goal a place in your week. You need to write the plan. You need to do the first task.

This worksheet is your working page. It is not a place for inspiration. It is a place for decisions. Use it to turn the goal from a cloud into a project. By the time you finish, you should be able to say: “Here is the goal. Here is the deadline. Here are the milestones. Here are the dates. Here are the actions. Here is the first task I will do this week.”

## A: Assign one goal

Pick one big goal that matters to you. Write it in clear terms with a number and a deadline. If the goal is about a relationship, write what success looks like. This step stops you from chasing many goals at once.

For A, write one goal only. Do not list five goals. Do not write a category. Do not write a feeling. Write the target. The goal should be specific enough that another person could read it and understand what you are trying to achieve. It should also be measurable enough that you can tell whether you are moving toward it.

If the goal is financial, write the amount and the deadline. If the goal is physical, write the distance, weight, time, or performance target and the deadline. If the goal is business, write the launch date, revenue target, client count, or product milestone and the deadline. If the goal is relational, write the behavior and the time frame.

The goal should be big enough to matter, but clear enough to act on.

## U: Unpack the goal into milestones

Break the goal into 3 to 5 smaller targets. Ask what must be true at the halfway point and at the quarter points. This step turns a big dream into checkpoints.

For U, write 3 to 5 milestones that make the goal reachable. Each milestone should be smaller than the final goal. Each milestone should be clear enough that you can say yes or no. You should be able to look at a milestone and know whether you hit it or missed it.

Think of the final goal as the finish line. The milestones are the checkpoints along the road. They are not the whole goal. They are the proof that the goal is moving.

Ask yourself:

- What must be true at the first quarter point?
- What must be true at the halfway point?
- What must be true at the final quarter point?
- What must be true before the final deadline?

If the goal is financial, the milestones should be amounts of money. If the goal is physical, the milestones should be distances, weights, times, or performance levels. If the goal is business, the milestones should be completed pieces of work. If the goal is relational, the milestones should be repeated behaviors over time.

Do not write milestones that are too vague to measure. Do not write milestones that are too large to be useful. A milestone should be a checkpoint, not another dream.

## D: Date each milestone

Put a deadline on every milestone. A milestone without a date is just a wish. Write the date next to each target. Then work backward to the present. Ask what must be done this week to hit the first milestone. This step gives you a clear path from today to the finish line.

For D, add a date to every milestone. The date should be before the final deadline. The dates should create a path from today to the finish line. A milestone without a date is not a checkpoint. It is a hope.

After you date the milestones, work backward to the present. Look at the first milestone. Ask what must be done this week to hit it. Write that first task. The first task should be small enough to do in one sitting. It should be specific enough that you do not have to guess what it means.

This is where the plan becomes real. The goal is no longer something you think about. It is something you can do this week.

If the first milestone is “$1,000 by March 31,” the first task may be “Move $500 on the 1st.” If the first milestone is “Run 3 miles by April 1,” the first task may be “Run 20 minutes three times this week.” If the first milestone is “Call my brother every Sunday for month 1,” the first task may be “Put the Sunday call in my calendar.”

The first task does not have to be dramatic. It has to be done.

## I: Insert the actions into your week

Under each milestone, list the tasks or habits you need. Put these tasks into your calendar or weekly plan. This step makes the goal part of your normal life, not an extra wish.

For I, list the actions that move each milestone forward. These are not vague intentions. They are tasks or habits. They should be specific enough to schedule. They should be small enough to repeat. They should be the kind of actions you can do even on a hard week.

Under each milestone, write the actions that support it. If the milestone is financial, the actions may be transfers, spending limits, budget checks, or income tasks. If the milestone is physical, the actions may be workouts, rest days, meal prep, or tracking. If the milestone is business, the actions may be writing, design, outreach, or product work. If the milestone is relational, the actions may be calls, texts, dates, or scheduled conversations.

After you list the actions, put them into your calendar or weekly plan. Give them a day and a time. Treat them like appointments. If the action has no place in your week, it will lose to the things that do.

The goal is not an extra wish. It is part of your normal life.

## T: Track and adjust weekly

Set a short review time, like Sunday evening. Check what you completed, what you missed, and what the numbers show. If you are on track, keep going. If you are off track, change one action or move one milestone. This step completes the lesson by turning your plan into a living system that keeps you moving.

For T, write your weekly review time. Make it short. Make it regular. Make it non-negotiable. A good review time is 15 to 20 minutes. It should be a fixed appointment, not something you do when you remember.

During the review, check three things:

- What did you complete?
- What did you miss?
- What do the numbers show?

The numbers are your proof. They may be dollars saved, miles run, pounds lost, words written, clients contacted, calls made, or tasks completed. The numbers tell you whether the plan is working. They also tell you whether the plan is too hard or too easy.

If you are on track, keep going. Do not overthink it. Do not restart the plan. Do not add five new actions. Keep the system moving.

If you are off track, change one action or move one milestone. Do not rewrite the whole plan. Do not quit. Do not call it a failure. Find the missed action and put it back into the next week. If the milestone is impossible, move the date. If the action is too large, make it smaller. If the action is too easy, add a little more.

The weekly review is what keeps the goal alive. It turns the plan from a one-time worksheet into a living system.

## Finish and Act

When the worksheet is complete, you should be able to read it and know exactly what you are doing. You should see one goal. You should see a deadline. You should see 3 to 5 milestones. You should see a date next to each milestone. You should see the actions that support each milestone. You should see the first task for this week. You should see the weekly review time.

That is the plan.

The plan is not perfect. It does not need to be. It needs to be written. It needs to be dated. It needs to be scheduled. It needs to be reviewed.

Do the first task. If you can do it today, do it today. If you cannot do it today, do it before the day you set. Treat it like an appointment. Put it in your calendar. Give it a time. Complete it.

When you complete the first task, the goal stops being a wish. It becomes a project. You have a number. You have a date. You have a milestone. You have a first task done and a next task scheduled.

Your goal is not waiting for you to feel ready. It is waiting for a date, a milestone, and a first task. Make them now.
