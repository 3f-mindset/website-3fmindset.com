# Title

Lock In the Three Changes That Matter Most

# Subtitle

Turn weeks of honest review into a simple plan that improves your daily life and keeps your gains from fading.

# Promise

Create a written three-part improvement plan in 30 minutes, without changing your whole life, cutting off every difficult person, or adding hours of work to your week.

# Motivations

- Build a daily setting that makes good choices easier.
- Keep the best changes discovered during recent self-audits.
- Spend more time on the people and duties that matter.
- Reduce habits and influences that weaken progress.
- Enter the next phase with three clear priorities already in motion.

# Challenges & Sacrifices

- You have many notes but do not know which issue to address first.
- Work and family duties leave little time for major life changes.
- Some relationships cannot be changed or ended quickly.
- New habits compete with routines you have followed for years.
- Large changes, such as a career move, may take months or years.

# Frustrations

- You keep learning useful lessons but fail to use them.
- The same weak habits return after a few good days.
- Your notes grow while your daily life stays the same.
- You waste effort trying to fix too many things at once.
- You know what needs to change but have no clear way to track it.

# Transformations

- From collecting ideas to acting on three clear priorities.
- From short bursts of effort to repeatable weekly actions.
- From a long list of problems to a short written plan.
- From reacting to old habits to using clear rules.
- From hoping life improves to tracking visible proof that it is changing.

# Choice

You can keep reviewing the same problems while your old setting, routines, and influences keep producing the same results.

That is the hard way.

Or you can choose the three changes that matter most, turn each one into a clear rule, and begin proving through action that your life is moving in a better direction.

# Actionable VERB

## CEMENT

Use **CEMENT** to turn your strongest insights into changes that hold.

# Model

## C: Collect the Evidence

Review your notes from the last several weeks.

Look for facts, not vague opinions.

Write down what you learned about:

- Your physical and digital setting
- Your daily habits
- Your closest relationships
- Your beliefs
- Your values
- Your roles and use of time

Good answers name a clear cause and result.

Examples:

- “Keeping my phone beside the bed leads to 30 minutes of scrolling each morning.”
- “I miss family time because I answer work messages during dinner.”
- “I train more often when my gym clothes are ready the night before.”

Avoid statements such as:

- “I need to do better.”
- “My life feels off.”
- “Everything needs work.”

You need facts you can act on.

## E: Evaluate the Impact

Review each item and ask:

- How often does this affect me?
- What does it cost me?
- What improves when I handle it well?
- Will changing it help more than one area of life?

Give each item a score from 1 to 5.

Use these three measures:

- Frequency
- Cost
- Reach

A phone habit may affect sleep, focus, work, and family. That gives it wide reach.

A small issue that happens once a month may not belong in your top three.

Choose based on impact, not ease.

## M: Mark the Top Three

Select three changes that deserve your attention now.

Each one should be important enough to improve daily life and clear enough to act on.

Your three choices may come from different areas:

- Home or work setting
- Relationships
- Health
- Time use
- Beliefs
- Family
- Personal duties

Do not choose ten.

A short plan followed well beats a long plan ignored.

Write one sentence for each priority:

- “I will remove my phone from the bedroom.”
- “I will protect three training times each week.”
- “I will have device-free dinners with my family.”

## E: Establish the Rules

Turn each priority into a clear rule.

Each rule must include:

- The action
- The time or trigger
- The number or frequency
- The proof of completion

Example:

“Every Friday at 4:00 p.m., I will spend 15 minutes clearing my desk. I will mark it complete on my weekly tracker.”

Another example:

“During dinner, my phone will remain in another room at least five nights each week.”

Avoid rules such as:

- “Be more present.”
- “Keep things cleaner.”
- “Spend less time online.”

These are wishes, not instructions.

A useful rule tells you exactly what to do.

## N: Name the Support

Decide what will help each change continue.

Support may include:

- A calendar reminder
- A checklist
- A prepared space
- A spouse or friend
- A weekly review
- A removed distraction
- A visible copy of the plan

Match the support to the problem.

If you forget, use reminders.

If you avoid the action, use accountability.

If the setting works against you, change the setting.

If the task feels too large, make the first action smaller.

Write one support beside each of your three rules.

## T: Track the First Proof

Choose the first visible sign that each improvement is working.

Examples:

- Five mornings without phone use in bed
- Three completed workouts in one week
- Four device-free family dinners
- One week with a clear desk
- Two fewer hours of social media use
- One hard conversation completed
- Seven days of following a new belief through action

Track results for seven days.

At the end of the week, ask:

- Did I follow the rule?
- Did it improve the problem?
- What blocked me?
- What needs to change in the plan?

Keep what works.

Change what does not.

Do not drop the goal just because the first method failed.

The lesson is complete when your top three priorities are written, scheduled, supported, and started.

# Action Steps Summary

## 1. Choose the Three

Review your recent notes and select the three changes with the greatest effect on your daily life.

## 2. Write the Rules

Turn each change into a clear action with a time, frequency, and way to prove completion.

## 3. Start the Scorecard

Begin now, track seven days of action, and update the plan using real results.

# FAQ

## 1. Why do I keep learning lessons but never changing?

You may be collecting insight without turning it into a scheduled action. Choose one rule, give it a time, and track it.

## 2. Why do my good habits disappear after a few days?

The habit may depend on memory or mood. Add a trigger, reminder, and visible scorecard.

## 3. Why does my life still feel crowded after I make changes?

You may be adding better actions without removing weak ones. Cut one distraction or duty that blocks the new plan.

## 4. How do I deal with a problem that may take months to fix?

Set the direction, then choose the next action you can complete this week. Long changes still need short steps.

## 5. How do I know which problem to handle first?

Choose the issue that happens often, carries a real cost, and affects several parts of your life.
