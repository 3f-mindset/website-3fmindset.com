# SORT: A Seven-Day Priority Experiment

This worksheet is not a prettier to-do list. It is a decision surface. Use it once to clear the noise, then repeat the daily page for seven days. Write specific actions, not identities or intentions.

## S: Survey the Full Load

Write every open loop: work, home, health, money, relationships, errands, and ideas. Do not organize while you capture.

Avoid: “get life together,” “be productive,” or other fog.

Better: “book the dentist,” “send the revised proposal,” “buy groceries for Thursday.”

Finish when every task currently pulling at your attention is visible somewhere outside your head.

## O: Order by Consequence

Mark each task:

- **A:** important and consequential this week
- **B:** useful support work
- **C:** optional, low consequence, or easy to batch

Then choose the three A-level tasks that would make today count. If you choose eight, you have not prioritized.

Avoid: choosing tasks because they are quick or because someone else asked loudly.

Better: choose the work tied to a real obligation, goal, or cost of delay.

Finish when three big rocks are written as observable outcomes.

## R: Reserve Prime Time

Put each big rock into a real block on the calendar. Name the start, end, and location. Add one protection rule: phone away, door closed, notifications off, or a clear handoff to someone else.

Avoid: “I’ll get to it sometime tomorrow.”

Better: “9:00–9:45, desk, draft the first three proposal sections.”

Finish when the calendar contains a first focused block and a realistic place for the other rocks.

## T: Terminate the Rest

For every task that does not deserve prime attention, make a decision: delete it, defer it to a date, delegate it with a clear request, or batch it with similar work.

Avoid: carrying every task forward unchanged.

Better: “Delete old research,” “delegate invoice check to Sam by Wednesday,” or “batch calls from 4:00–4:30.”

Finish when no task remains on the list without an owner, time, or deliberate decision.

## Daily Review

At the end of the day, record the three rocks, what moved, what interrupted you, and the one change you will make tomorrow. Do not use the review to punish yourself. Use it to improve the next decision.

The seven-day finish condition is simple: you have seven daily sheets, a visible pattern of what receives your attention, and one change you will keep.
